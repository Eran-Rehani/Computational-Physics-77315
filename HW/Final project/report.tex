\begin{abstract}
    I simulate 5000 equal-mass particles moving under their own gravity
    for \SI{20}{Gyr}, at three starting speeds, $V=65$, $80$ and
    \SI{95}{km/s}. The forces come from a Barnes-Hut octree with the
    assigned $\theta=1$ test on the cell diagonal and the assigned
    softened force, and I step the particles forward with adaptive
    fourth-order Runge-Kutta, comparing one full step against two half
    steps.

    The assigned accuracy, $\varepsilon=0.1$ of the box size, allows a
    step error of \SI{133.2}{kpc}. That is larger than the cluster's
    starting diameter of \SI{100}{kpc}, so it barely limits the step size
    and I ran everything twice. Appendix~\ref{sec:appendixeps} gives every
    required output at $\varepsilon=0.1$, and the main text repeats the
    three runs with a tolerance $10^3$ times tighter. I quote the energy
    error as $\max_t\abs{\Delta E/E_0}$ of the escaper-corrected energy,
    which reaches $54\%$ at the assigned tolerance and stays below $0.7\%$
    at the production tolerance, so the physical conclusions come from the
    production runs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem and initial conditions}\label{sec:problem}

The system is $N=5000$ equal-mass particles ($m = \num{2e7}\,M_\odot$, total mass $M=\num{1e11}\,M_\odot$) moving under their own gravity for \SI{20}{Gyr}. At $t=0$ they fill a sphere of radius $R=\SI{50}{kpc}$ uniformly. To sample that sphere I draw three uniform random variables $u_r, u_\mu, u_\varphi \sim U(0,1)$ and map them to spherical coordinates:
\begin{equation}
    r = R u_r^{1/3},
    \qquad
    \cos\vartheta = 2u_\mu - 1,
    \qquad
    \varphi = 2\pi u_\varphi.
\end{equation}
The cube root is what makes the sample uniform in volume rather than in radius. The volume element is $r^2\,dr$, so drawing $r$ flat on $[0,R]$ would crowd particles into the center. With $u_r^{1/3}$ the number density comes out constant,
\begin{equation}
    \rho = \frac{M}{\frac{4\pi}{3}R^3}.
\end{equation}

Each Cartesian velocity component is Gaussian with standard deviation $\sigma = V/\sqrt{3}$, which sets the initial rms speed to $V = \sqrt{\langle v^2\rangle}$. The assignment asks for three cases, $V = 65$, $80$ and $\SI{95}{km/s}$. I draw the positions once and the velocities once, then reuse both draws in all three runs and rescale the velocities by $V$, so any difference between the runs comes from the starting kinetic energy and not from the random numbers. I subtract the mean velocity to remove the bulk motion of the cluster. I leave the positions alone, because shifting them can push particles outside the sphere the assignment specifies.

Particles interact via softened gravity:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:softforce}
    \mathbf{f}_{ij}=-\frac{Gm_im_j}{(r_{ij}+S)^2}
    \frac{\mathbf{r}_{ij}}{r_{ij}},
    \qquad \mathbf{r}_{ij}=\mathbf{r}_i-\mathbf{r}_j,
\end{equation}
with softening length $S=\SI{1}{kpc}$. Softening does two things here. It caps the pair acceleration at $|a_{ij}| \le G m_j / S^2$ instead of letting it diverge as $r_{ij} \to 0$, so one close approach cannot collapse the iteration on its own. It also stops particles from knocking each other off course when they pass too close. The 5000 simulation particles represent a smooth, collisionless mass distribution, so these close encounters are only a side effect of the simulation.

The potential energy that goes with this force is
\begin{equation}
    U_{ij}=-\frac{Gm_im_j}{r_{ij}+S}
\end{equation}
This potential matches the softened force in Equation~\ref{eq:softforce}, so the energy calculation is consistent with the simulation. After each accepted step I drop the particles that have left the cubic box $|x|,|y|,|z| \le \SI{666}{kpc}$ ($L_{\mathrm{box}} = \SI{1332}{kpc}$).

\section{Numerical method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Units}

I measure everything in kpc, $M_\odot$ and Gyr, which keeps the numerical values convenient. In these units,
\begin{equation}
    G=\SI{4.4996e-6}{kpc^3.M_\odot^{-1}.Gyr^{-2}},
    \qquad \SI{1}{kpc/Gyr}=\SI{0.9777922}{km/s}.
\end{equation}
Each simulation particle has mass $m=M/N=\num{2e7}\,M_\odot$.

\subsection{Barnes-Hut tree}

Forces come from a Barnes-Hut octree \cite{barneshut}. The particles go into a cubic root cell. Any cell holding more than one particle splits into eight octants, and the splitting stops when a cell holds at most one. Every cell stores its total mass $M_{\mathrm{cell}}$, its center of mass $\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{cm}}$, its particle count and its diagonal $L = \sqrt{3}\,l$. The tree pays off because I can replace a distant clump by one point mass at its center of mass, which brings a single force evaluation from $O(N^2)$ down to $O(N\log N)$.

For a given target particle, the tree search always opens a cell that contains the target, and it evaluates leaf cells directly. For a cell the target lies outside, at distance $D$ from its center of mass, the algorithm recurses when $D/L < \theta$ and otherwise lumps the cell into a single point mass $M_{\mathrm{cell}}$ at $\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{cm}}$. The assignment defines the opening test using the cell diagonal $L = \sqrt{3}\,l$, while the usual definition uses the side length $l$. The two definitions are related by
\begin{equation}
    \frac{D}{L} \ge \theta_{\mathrm{diag}} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \frac{l}{D} \le \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}\,\theta_{\mathrm{diag}}} \equiv \theta_{\mathrm{side}}.
\end{equation}
so the assigned $\theta_{\mathrm{diag}} = 1$ is not the familiar $\theta = 1$. It is $\theta_{\mathrm{side}} = 1/\sqrt{3} \approx 0.577$, which opens more cells than the usual choice, so it is both more accurate and slower. I rebuild the tree at every Runge-Kutta stage because the particle positions change between stages.

Dividing Equation~\ref{eq:softforce} by the target mass gives the acceleration from one source particle or from a cell treated as a single mass:
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf{a}_{ij}=-\frac{Gm_j}{(r_{ij}+S)^2}
 \frac{\mathbf{r}_{ij}}{r_{ij}}.
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:tree} illustrates the method using a two-dimensional quadtree. It works like an octree but is easier to visualize. For 60 particles, the target requires 12 direct particle terms and 14 cell terms, giving 26 terms instead of 59.

\subsection{Adaptive RK4 integration}

The state vector $\mathbf{y}=(\mathbf{r}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{r}_N, \mathbf{v}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{v}_N)^\top$ contains all particle positions and velocities. The equations of motion are $\dot{\mathbf{r}}_i=\mathbf{v}_i$ and $\dot{\mathbf{v}}_i=\mathbf{a}_i$. I control the RK4 step size using step doubling. For each proposed step $h$, I compare one full step with two half steps over the same time interval. Both calculations start from the same state and share the first stage $\mathbf{k}_1$. Reusing this stage reduces each proposed step from 12 force evaluations to 11 without changing the result.

The error estimate is the largest displacement difference over single particles:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:errnorm}
    \mathrm{err}=\max_{1\le i\le N}\norm{\mathbf{r}_{i,\mathrm{half}}
        -\mathbf{r}_{i,\mathrm{full}}}.
\end{equation}
I measure the error for each particle rather than across the full $6N$ state vector. I do this because the assigned tolerance is a distance and should be compared with one particle's position error. When $\mathrm{err}\le\mathrm{tol}$ the step is accepted, and the two estimates are then combined by Richardson extrapolation:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{acc}}=\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{half}}+
    \frac{\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{half}}-\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{full}}}{2^4-1},
\end{equation}
This removes the main $O(h^5)$ error without any extra calculations. The next step size is then
\begin{equation}
    h_{\mathrm{next}} = h \times 0.9 \left(\frac{\mathrm{tol}}{\mathrm{err}}\right)^{1/5},
\end{equation}
I limit each step-size change to a factor between 0.1 and 2, never shrink a rejected step below $0.01\,\mathrm{Gyr}$, and never grow one past $2\,\mathrm{Gyr}$. The lower limit prevents a close particle pair from making the step size so small that the simulation stalls. A step can still come out shorter than that when it is truncated to land exactly on an output time, and for the few steps that follow such a truncation while $h$ grows back.

The assignment fixes the tolerance at $\varepsilon = 0.1$ of the box size:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tolliteral}
    \mathrm{tol}=\varepsilon L_{\mathrm{box}}=0.1\times\SI{1332}{kpc}=\SI{133.2}{kpc}.
\end{equation}
This tolerance is larger than the cluster's initial diameter of \SI{100}{kpc}. A step can therefore pass the test even when the particle positions are inaccurate. For $V=\SI{80}{km/s}$ the energy error then reaches $54\%$, against $0.4\%$ at the production tolerance. Section~\ref{sec:tolerance} explains that choice of \SI{0.1332}{kpc}, and results using the assigned value are given in full in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendixeps}.

\subsection{Energy checks and escaper accounting}

At each accepted step the code evaluates
\begin{equation}\label{eq:energy}
    E_k=\frac12\sum_i m_i v_i^2,
    \qquad E_g=\frac12\sum_i m_i\Phi_i,
    \qquad \Phi_i=-G\sum_{j\ne i}\frac{m_j}{r_{ij}+S}.
\end{equation}
For $N>1500$, I calculate the potentials $\Phi_i$ using the tree. For smaller systems, I use the exact direct sum because it is fast enough and avoids tree approximation errors.

When a particle leaves the box I drop it from the system, and its kinetic and potential energy go with it. The total energy then jumps, even though nothing physical has happened. To tell that jump apart from integration error, I record the particle's energy at the moment it leaves. The retained energy includes only the remaining particles, while the corrected energy adds the recorded energies of all escaped particles:
\begin{equation}
    E_{\mathrm{corrected}}(t) = E_{\mathrm{retained}}(t) + \sum_{k \in \mathrm{escapers}} E_{\mathrm{esc}, k}.
\end{equation}
I remove a particle as soon as it crosses the box boundary, even if it is still bound to the cluster. For $V=80\text{ km/s}$, 167 particles leave in 123 removal events. Of those, 21 have a total recorded energy at or below zero, $E_k+E_g\leq0$.

\subsection{Density profile and King fit}

I measure each radius from the center of mass of the remaining particles and divide the particles into 25 logarithmically spaced shells. This gives smaller shells in the dense core and larger shells in the sparse outer region. For a shell with inner radius $r_j$ and outer radius $r_{j+1}$, I divide the shell mass $M_j$ by its volume to find the density. I plot this density at the geometric midpoint of the shell:
\begin{equation}
    \rho_j=\frac{M_j}{\frac{4\pi}{3}(r_{j+1}^3-r_j^3)},
    \qquad M_j = N_j\,m,
    \qquad r_{j,\mathrm{plot}}=\sqrt{r_jr_{j+1}},
\end{equation}
where $N_j$ is the number of particles in the shell. Since the particles have equal masses, counting them gives the Poisson uncertainty $\delta\rho_j = \rho_j / \sqrt{N_j}$. The geometric midpoint fits here because both the shells and the radius axis are logarithmic.

I then fit the profile to the King model,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:king}
    \rho(r)=\frac{\rho_c}{[1+(r/r_c)^\alpha]^\beta},
\end{equation}
Here, $\rho_c$ is the central density and $r_c$ is the core radius. At large radii, the density falls as $\rho(r) \propto r^{-\alpha\beta}$, so the outer slope depends on the product $\alpha\beta$. I fit $\log\rho$ rather than $\rho$ because the density spans several orders of magnitude. Fitting $\rho$ itself would let the dense inner bins take over the fit. I weight each bin by $1/\delta\log\rho \approx \sqrt{N_j}$.

The assignment asks for the same $\alpha$ and $\beta$ in all three cases, and allows two ways to do this. One way fits all four parameters to the $V=80\text{ km/s}$ run and uses its $\alpha$ and $\beta$ for the other two. The other way fits the shared shape to all three runs at once, and that is the one I use. The three profiles go into a single least-squares problem with eight free parameters, a separate $\rho_c$ and $r_c$ for each velocity and one $\alpha$ and one $\beta$ for all of them. The fit adds the $\chi^2$ of the three profiles and minimizes the sum, so the three profiles pick the shape together. Taking the shape from $V=80$ alone instead pushes the other two runs onto an outer slope that does not match their data. I ran both, since either one meets the requirement. Fitting the shape jointly lowers the total $\chi^2$ from $1428$ to $1166$ over the same 65 degrees of freedom. Most of the gain is in the $V=95\text{ km/s}$ run, the worst case before, whose $\chi^2$ drops from $984$ to $508$. Running \texttt{nbody\_solver.py -{}-refit} prints both.

\section{Implementation and checks}\label{sec:implementation}

I wrote the code in \texttt{nbody\_solver.py}. To make it fast enough I store
the tree in flattened arrays and let Numba compile the tree search, and I walk
the tree for the target particles in parallel across threads, which is what
makes the $N=5000$ runs over \SI{20}{Gyr} practical. The RK controller is the
one I wrote for HW\_05, changed to use the per-particle norm of
Equation~\ref{eq:errnorm} and to resize the state vector when particles are
removed. The isotropic direction sampler comes from HW\_07.

Running \texttt{python nbody\_solver.py -{}-test} executes the checks described
below, and \texttt{-{}-tol=X} runs all three $N=5000$ cases. I write the output
to \texttt{figures/}, and there are also redraw and refit modes that rebuild the
plots and the fitted tables from the saved states.

I first test the sampler using 5000 particles. The test sample has a maximum
radius of \SI{49.9905}{kpc} and an RMS speed of \SI{79.422}{km/s}, while the
production sample gives \SI{49.9961}{kpc} and \SI{80.235}{km/s}. Both maximum
radii are below $R=\SI{50}{kpc}$, and both speeds are within $1\%$ of the target
$V=\SI{80}{km/s}$, as expected for a finite random sample. After subtracting
the mean velocity, the center-of-mass velocity is zero to machine precision.

I check the tree against direct summation. For 150 particles with
unequal masses at $\theta=1$, the Barnes-Hut acceleration has a relative error
$\norm{\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{tree}}-\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{dir}}}/
    \norm{\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{dir}}}=1.014\times10^{-2}$, with both norms stacked
over all particles and components. The tree gravitational energy differs from
the direct result by $5.214\times10^{-4}$.

I test the integrator using problems with known solutions. I evolve a softened,
unequal-mass circular binary for two orbits with a tight tolerance, and its
relative energy change is only $1.29\times10^{-10}$, with a final separation
that matches its initial value. A constant-velocity test also confirms that
each snapshot is saved at the correct time.

For reference, the opening condition with $L=\sqrt3\,l$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:thetaconv}
    \frac{D}{L}\geq\theta_{\mathrm{diagonal}}
    \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
    \frac{l}{D}\leq
    \frac{1}{\sqrt3\,\theta_{\mathrm{diagonal}}}
    \equiv\theta_{\mathrm{side}}.
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{tab:theta} compares the tree acceleration with a direct sum for
3000 particles from the initial sphere. At the assigned $\theta=1$, the $L_2$
error is $9.14\times10^{-3}$, similar to the result of the smaller random test.

Using $\theta=2$ reduces the error by about a factor of eight with little extra
runtime, because for $N=5000$ most of the time goes into building the tree, so
checking more cells adds little cost. The production runs use the assigned

With the production tolerance, the controller accepted 310, 262 and 352 steps
for $V=80$, $95$ and \SI{65}{km/s}, and rejected 4, 10 and 3 proposed steps. The
corresponding runs at the assigned tolerance rejected no steps, and the trial
run of Table~\ref{tab:tolerance} rejects one, because it uses a smaller minimum
step and no intermediate output times. This shows that the assigned tolerance is
too loose to control the step size. The estimated error almost always remains
below the large tolerance, so the controller accepts coarse steps and allows $h$
to grow toward its maximum value. The simulation then uses too few steps to
follow the particle motion accurately.

\section{Choice of integration tolerance}\label{sec:tolerance}

The assigned tolerance, \SI{133.2}{kpc}, is larger than the initial cluster
radius, so I could not tell by inspection how much of the energy drift was
physical and how much was integration error. To answer this, I repeated the
$V=\SI{80}{km/s}$ simulation using four tolerances that span three orders of
magnitude.
For each run, the corrected energy is the retained energy plus the energy
recorded for particles when they escape. This removes the jumps caused by
deleting particles, but the force and energy still contain the same Barnes-Hut
approximation at every tolerance. Since only the tolerance changes between
runs, the differences mainly show integration error.

Within Table~\ref{tab:tolerance}, tightening the tolerance by $10^3$ lowers the

four trial runs use a smaller minimum step and no intermediate output times, so
their numbers sit slightly below the production values quoted elsewhere ($54\%$
and $0.4\%$ for the same two tolerances at $V=\SI{80}{km/s}$). The trend is the

tolerance under the suffix \texttt{\_eps01}.

The drift is still falling at the last row of Table~\ref{tab:tolerance}, so it
has not yet reached a lower limit. With an even tighter tolerance, errors from
the tree and close encounters would eventually take over, but these runs do not
reach that point. I stopped because the remaining drift was already much smaller
than the physical effects I was measuring.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Initial energies and virial state}

Before the simulations, I estimate whether each cluster starts above or below
virial equilibrium. The virial theorem states that a self-gravitating system
in equilibrium satisfies $2E_k+E_g=0$, or $2E_k=\abs{E_g}$. For an
unsoftened uniform sphere, $E_g=-\frac{3}{5}\frac{GM^2}{R}$ and
$E_k=\frac12 MV^2$. Therefore, the equilibrium speed is
\begin{equation}
    V_{\mathrm{vir}}=\sqrt{\frac{3GM}{5R}}=\SI{71.85}{km/s}.
\end{equation}
The assigned speeds lie on both sides of this value, and that is why the three
runs start out so differently.
\begin{itemize}
    \item $V=65\text{ km/s}$ is sub-virial, $2E_{k,0}/\abs{E_{g,0}} = 0.849$. There is not enough kinetic energy to hold the sphere up, so it collapses straight away.
    \item $V=80\text{ km/s}$ is super-virial, $2E_{k,0}/\abs{E_{g,0}} = 1.286$. The excess kinetic energy pushes the outer layers out before gravity pulls the system back.
    \item $V=95\text{ km/s}$ is strongly super-virial, $2E_{k,0}/\abs{E_{g,0}} = 1.814$. Its total energy is only just negative, so it is close to unbinding altogether and expands fast.
\end{itemize}
All three still start bound, $E_0 < 0$ (Table~\ref{tab:runs}), so none of them simply disperses.

\subsection{(b) Energy conservation}

Figure~\ref{fig:energy} shows the relative energy change
$\Delta E/E_0=(E(t)-E_0)/E_0$ for $V=\SI{80}{km/s}$. Since $E_0$ is
negative, a positive value means that the total energy has become more
negative. The retained energy (blue) and corrected energy (orange) agree until
the first particle leaves the box at $t=\SI{5.3}{Gyr}$.

During the \SI{20}{Gyr} simulation, 167 particles escape. They remove kinetic
energy equal to 4.6\% of $\abs{E_0}$, but only 0.8\% in interaction energy
because they are already far from the cluster. Removing them therefore makes
the remaining system appear more tightly bound, and the retained curve rises
to $+0.034$. Adding their recorded energies back reduces the drift to
$-0.0039$, or 0.39\%. The effect is larger for $V=95\text{ km/s}$, where 758
particles escape. In that run, the retained curve reaches $+0.81$, while the
corrected curve ends at $+0.0013$.

\subsection{(c) Virial ratio and violent relaxation}

For $V=80\text{ km/s}$, the ratio $-2E_k/E_g$ falls from $1.286$ to
$0.82$ during the first \SI{2.5}{Gyr}. It then oscillates around 1 with a
decreasing amplitude (Figure~\ref{fig:virial}), with peaks about
\SI{4.5}{Gyr} apart. This is a global breathing mode. The cluster expands past
its equilibrium size, contracts again, and repeats with smaller oscillations.

The damping is a sign of violent relaxation \cite{lyndenbell}. When a
collisionless system sits far from equilibrium, its gravitational potential
$\Phi(\mathbf{r},t)$ changes fast. Single particles then do not keep their own
energy, and the changing potential moves them onto new orbits within one
dynamical time, about $\tau_{\mathrm{dyn}}\sim\SI{2.5}{Gyr}$ here. That
reshuffles the system far faster than close encounters between pairs of
particles could. Once the potential settles, particle energies hold steady
again, violent relaxation ends, and the oscillations fade.

At $t=\SI{20}{Gyr}$, the retained ratios are $0.960$, $0.954$ and $0.937$
for $V=65$, $80$ and $\SI{95}{km/s}$. Values below 1 do not have to mean the
clusters are out of equilibrium. The relation
$-2E_k/E_g=1$ is exact only for an unsoftened $1/r$ potential. Softening changes
the potential to $U_{ij}\propto(r_{ij}+S)^{-1}$, so the virial term
$\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{f}$ is no longer equal to the potential energy $U$.
The general equilibrium condition is therefore $2E_k+W=0$, where $W$ is the
Clausius virial \cite{goldstein,binneytremaine}:
\begin{equation}
    W = \sum_{i < j} \mathbf{r}_{ij} \cdot \mathbf{f}_{ij} = -\sum_{i < j} \frac{G m_i m_j r_{ij}}{(r_{ij}+S)^2}.
\end{equation}
At the final time, $2E_k/\abs{W}$ is $0.9996$, $0.982$ and $0.956$, which
\texttt{nbody\_solver.py -{}-virial} prints from the saved states.
These values are closer to 1, confirming that the clusters are near virial
equilibrium. The figure uses the ratio required by the assignment, while these
corrected values account for softening.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{figures/fig_c.pdf}
    \caption{Virial ratio for $V=\SI{80}{km/s}$. Blue uses the retained
        particles only, orange adds each escaper's energy recorded at removal,
        and the dashed line marks $-2E_k/E_g=1$.}
    \label{fig:virial}
\end{figure}

\subsection{(d) Density profiles and King fits}

Figure~\ref{fig:density} shows the final radial density profiles and their King
fits. As the initial speed increases, the core radius grows from $22.9$ to
$32.3$ to \SI{50.8}{kpc}, while the central density falls from
$8.3\times10^5$ to $6.4\times10^4\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{kpc}^{-3}$. Faster
particles travel farther from the center and spend more time at large radii,
producing a wider and less dense core.

        fits. All three curves share one $\alpha$ and one $\beta$, fitted from the

        $\mathrm{tol}=\SI{0.1332}{kpc}$. The uncertainties are

    \label{tab:king}
    \input{figures/king_table.tex}

The shared shape parameters are $\alpha=4.50\pm0.22$ and
$\beta=0.983\pm0.053$. At large radii the density therefore falls as
$\rho(r)\propto r^{-\alpha\beta}=r^{-4.43}$ for all three velocities, which is
what makes the three fitted curves in Figure~\ref{fig:density} parallel at
large $r$. For comparison, fixing $\alpha$ and $\beta$ at the values from a
four-parameter fit to $V=80$ alone gives $\alpha=4.83\pm0.38$,
$\beta=0.986\pm0.085$ and an outer slope of $4.76$. The two ways of fixing the
shape agree on the outer slope to within the uncertainty on $\alpha$.

The global $\chi^2$ per degree of freedom is $1166/65=17.9$. The three runs
contribute $256$, $508$ and $403$ for $V=80$, $95$ and \SI{65}{km/s}. These
numbers are large because the error bars are small. The Poisson uncertainty
falls as $\delta\rho\propto1/\sqrt{N}$, so a shell holding thousands of
particles has a tiny error bar, and even a small gap between the data and the
King curve then counts for a lot. The fit also covers the whole radial range,
past about \SI{200}{kpc}, where the shells hold escaping particles rather than
the settled cluster. The King model is not meant to describe those, which is
why the outermost $V=65\text{ km/s}$ points in Figure~\ref{fig:density} sit
well below their fitted curve. The $V=95\text{ km/s}$ run still contributes
the most after the joint fit, because of the dip in its central density that a
single shared shape cannot follow.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Difficulties}\label{sec:difficulties}

\subsection{Controlling long-term energy drift}

The main difficulty I faced was keeping the energy stable during the full
\SI{20}{Gyr} simulation. With the assigned tolerance of \SI{133.2}{kpc}, the
RK4 controller accepted steps that were too large, and the energy error for
$V=\SI{80}{km/s}$ reached $54\%$. I tested smaller tolerances and found that
\SI{0.1332}{kpc} reduced it to $0.4\%$, although it required many more
steps. Because the
assignment requires adaptive RK4, I solved the problem by using this tighter
tolerance rather than changing the integration method.

\subsection{Close encounters and duplicate coordinates}

Close encounters produce large accelerations, which is why the controller needs
a floor on the step size at all. Softening limits the acceleration to
$|a_{ij}|\le Gm_j/S^2$, and in the production runs that turned out to be enough.
The floor only controls the shrinking of a rejected step, and no run ever needed
it. Accepted steps ranged from $0.004$ to $0.11\,\mathrm{Gyr}$ across the three
velocities, with typical values near $0.07\,\mathrm{Gyr}$. The shortest steps
do not come from close encounters. They follow each output time, where the step
is cut short to land on the breakpoint, and the controller can then grow it back
by at most a factor of two per accepted step. A
more serious problem occurs when two particles have exactly the same position,
$r_{ij}=0$. The octree cannot separate them, so a simple tree builder would
continue splitting forever.

To prevent this, I stop splitting and store both particles in the same leaf.
They act as one combined mass on other particles, and I set their mutual force
to zero because its direction is undefined at $r_{ij}=0$. The initial sampler is
very unlikely to create duplicate positions, but this stops the recursion from
running forever if duplicates do occur.

\subsection{Potential evaluation and escaper energy}

I use the tree for the potential, which costs $O(N\log N)$ instead of $O(N^2)$.
At $N=150$ it is off by $5.2\times10^{-4}$ against a direct sum. This is much
smaller than the integration drift, so the tree is not the main source of error.

My escaper correction is also only approximate. Recording each particle's energy
when it leaves removes the false jumps in Figures~\ref{fig:energy} and
\ref{fig:virial}, but ignores later changes in its potential energy. This
missing contribution is small near the box boundary, but not exactly zero.
Therefore, the corrected curve is a useful check on the energy rather than an
exactly conserved quantity.

\textit{Related implementation.} I also looked at the open-source
\href{https://github.com/lechebs/nbody}{\texttt{lechebs/nbody}} project, which
runs a Barnes-Hut simulation in real time using CUDA C++ and OpenGL. I used it
only as a comparison with another implementation. All numerical results in this
report come from my CPU/Numba code.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The Barnes Hut forces agree with direct summation to about $1\%$ at
$\theta=1$, and the integrator passes the binary and constant-velocity tests.
By \SI{20}{Gyr}, all three clusters are close to virial equilibrium, with
$2E_k/\abs{W}\approx0.96$-$1.00$. The sub-virial $V=65\text{ km/s}$ cluster
contracts and retains $99.6\%$ of its mass. The $V=80$ and $95\text{ km/s}$
clusters expand and lose $3.3\%$ and $15.2\%$ of their mass.

The main change from the assignment is the tolerance. The assigned value of
\SI{133.2}{kpc} allows up to $54\%$ energy error, while the production value of
\SI{0.1332}{kpc} keeps it below $0.7\%$. Appendix~\ref{sec:appendixeps}
includes the results at the assigned tolerance for comparison. In the production
runs, a higher initial speed produces a larger core,
$r_c=22.9\to\SI{50.8}{kpc}$, and a lower central density.
